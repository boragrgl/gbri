 Introduction  
Over the past decade, Mexico’s financial system has experienced structural changes, driven by the emergence 
of non -bank financial institutions (NBFIs) and the rapid adoption of digital technologies. Among these, 
Sociedades Financieras Populares  (SOFIPOs), which are regulated deposit ‑taking NBFIs that are authorized to 
provide savings and credit services but operate under a simplified regulatory and supervisory framework 
distinct from that of commercial banks.  In particular, SOFIPOs face lower capital and activity requirements, 
focus primarily on small -scale and retail lending, and have increasingly relied on digital channels for credit 
origination . This position s them  at the intersection of traditional microfinance and FinTech innovation. This 
evolution has been reinforced by the 2018 FinTech Law, which established a formal regulatory framework for 
electronic payment institutions and crowdfunding platforms, while indirectly shaping the competitive landscape 
for both banks and SOFIPOs.  
While SOFIPOs remain a relatively small segment of Mexico’s financial system – accounting for 0.8 percent of 
total bank assets as of December 2024 – they serve a non -negligible fraction of the population. According to 
the Mexican National Banking and Secur ities Commission (CNBV, its acronym in Spanish), as of December 
2024, SOFIPOs have granted credit to approximately 10 million borrowers and serve approximately 15.4 million 
savers, totaling about 24.9 million clients – which is roughly 18 percent of the to tal population and about 32 
percent of banked individuals (i.e., CNBV, 2025). Thus, their growing role in digital intermediation raises 
important questions about competition, financial inclusion, and systemic risk. According to the Mexican Central 
Bank (Ba nxico, its acronym in Spanish) SOFIPOs have experienced the strongest growth in total assets among 
all NBFI entities in Mexico during the past three years (Banxico, 2025). The proliferation of Digital SOFIPOs (D -
SOFIPOs) has taken place in the context of l arge proportions of Mexico's population excluded by traditional 
banking services (i.e., Jagtiani and Lemieux, 2018; and Yang and Zhang, 2022). The landscape is further 
characterized by the relatively low level of private sector credit: total loans to the n on-financial private sector 
represent only 34.7 percent of GDP in Mexico. This figure is notably lower than the average observed among 
OECD member countries which stands at 151.8 percent (Banxico, 2025). It is worth noting that, while in the 
past, SOFIPOs were mostly located in regions/towns where  there was a low banking presence, far from urban 
areas and with higher operational costs, SOFIPOs adopting digital business models have substantially 
decreased operational costs while increasing access to a broader client base. In addition, the now lower entry 
cost for unregulated fintech lenders  to acquir e a SOFIPO license relative to a bank license has incentivized the 
incursion of new entities.  
In contrast to large commercial banks, D -SOFIPOs operate with lean capital structures and technology -driven 
business models. These features enable cost -efficient service delivery but may also introduce vulnerabilities, 
particularly under rapid scaling stra tegies and reliance on alternative credit scoring mechanisms. Such 
business models, while fostering inclusion and efficiency, may require careful regulatory oversight to guard 
against financial sector vulnerabilities as alternative digital lending institut ions expand their reach (IMF, 2025, 
Mexico Article IV Staff Report).  
In this paper, we examine the impact of NBFIs digital lending growth on banks’ risk and competition indicators, 
focusing on SOFIPOs specialized in digital lending, representing 78 percent of the total SOFIPO sector. In this 
sense, we contribute to the lite rature by empirically assessing whether the expansion of alternative lending 
channels by NBFIs alters risk -taking behavior and bank competition in the banking sector and affects systemic 
stability. Specifically, we test three hypotheses:

 H1: Competitive Pressures from Digital Lending: We posit that the growth of digital lending is associated with  
competitive pressures within the banking sector. As NBFIs expand their digital lending operations, traditional 
banks may experience intensified competition, potentially influencing their market behavior and strategies.  
H2: Expansion of Alternative Digital Lending and Bank Risk : Following the literature, we make the hypothesis  
that the expansion of alternative digital lending by NBFIs might lead to an increase in both individual bank risk 
and systemic risk. This premise considers the possibility that , as digital lending grows, the banking sector  may 
respond by increasing leverage to preserve profitability , which could also translate into heightened systemic 
vulnerability across the sector.  At the same time, changes in measured bank risk may  also reflect composition 
effect s, whereby borrower sorting toward non ‑bank digital lenders alters the risk profile of banks ’ remaining 
loan portfolios.  
H3: Impact of Digital NBFI Entry Across Bank Sizes: Finally, we test whether competitive and risk dynamics 
triggered by digital NBFIs are distributed uniformly among banks of different sizes, or if the effects are 
concentrated in specific segments of the banking sector.  Such asymmetry may reflect size ‑dependent 
differences in intermediation costs and borrower relationships.  
Annex I develops a simple, stylized model of competition between traditional banks and digital lenders to guide 
the interpretation of the empirical results. The theory we propose is deliberately parsimonious, and it serves as 
an organizing device to map the empirical hypotheses into a theoretical setting. Such a framework builds on the 
competition –risk literature (Keeley 1990; Boyd and D e Nicol ó 2005; Martinez -Miera and Repullo  2010) and 
recent work on FinTech entry (Philippon 2016) and highlights – in an interpretive, not structural way – how 
differences in operating structures between banks and digital lenders can affect pricing, borrower allocation, 
and bank ‑level outcomes.   
To our knowledge, this is the first empirical analysis exploring the nexus between alternative digital lending 
driven competition and financial stability, with a specific focus on Mexico. Our contribution is twofold:  
1. First, we use a modified Loan Expansion Index that explicitly incorporates D -SOFIPO penetration 
relative to the banking sector, allowing us to measure the expansion of alternative digital lending  within 
a highly concentrated banking sector such as Mexico  and its association with competitive dynamics . 
2. Second, we link this index to key systemic and banking stability indicators, such as the SRISK, Z -
score, non -performing loan ratio and net interest margins, to assess whether alternative lending 
channels complement or challenge systemic resilience.  
Our findings suggest that bank standalone risk -taking as measured by the Z -score decreases when market 
participation of D -SOFIPOs increases, while bank -level systemic risk contribution remains largely unaffected.  
Regarding competitive dynamics, our findings show that higher market participation of digital NBFIs is linked 
with an increase in competition pressures  captured by lower net interest margins . This intensification of 
competition is particularly pronounced among medium - and small -sized banks which experience the greatest 
impact following the entry of strategic  digital players such as Nu Bank México (Nu or Nu Bank for short) . Large 
banks, by contrast, appear to be largely insulated from these competitive pressures, likely due to economies of 
scale, greater cost efficiency, diversification,  and market share which grant them a higher degree of protection 
against the effects exerted by new digital entrants.  
Together, these findings suggest that the increasing presence of digital NBFIs may promote a more competitive 
environment for individual banks without compromising overall sector stability. We believe our results carry 
important implications for regulatory  design and macroprudential oversight. Insights from Mexico regarding how 
digital NBFIs interact with traditional banks could be critical for designing  competition policy and safeguarding

Related Literature  
The literature on the relationship between bank competition and solvency risk is primarily dominated by two 
hypotheses: the "competition -fragility" (Boyd and De Nicoló, 2005) and the "competition -stability" (Keeley, 
1990) hypotheses. The former hypothesis posits that increased competition (typically measured by the Lerner 
Index) may induce higher risk -taking by banks, thereby undermining individual stability as banks aim to keep 
profit margins. In contrast, the competition -stability hypothesis argues that c ompetition enhances individual 
stability by fostering efficiency and curbing monopolistic practices.  
The empirical evidence regarding the relationship between bank competition and individual bank stability 
remains inconclusive. Theoretical work by Martinez -Miera and Repullo (2010) advances the discussion by 
proposing that this relationship may exhibit a U -shaped pattern. According to their model, both very low and 
very high levels of competition can adversely affect solvency risk  (Cao et al., 2020). The literature expanded to 
analyze the trade -off between  competition and systemic risk arising from both correlation risk (e.g., Anginer et 
al., 2014; Hirata and Ojima, 2020; Leroy and Lucotte, 2017; Silva -Buston, 2019) and contagion risk (Bátiz -Zuk 
and Lara -Sánchez, 2023). Empirical studies have yielded mixed results, often contingent on institutional 
settings, regulatory frameworks, and market structures. In particular, the evidence is mixed for correlation risk 
metrics, whereas evidence for co ntagion metrics suggests that more competition increases systemic risk. 
Increased competition can affect a bank’s lending and financing decisions, which in turn may either heighten or 
mitigate systemic risk within the banking sector. Therefore, understandi ng the precise nature of this linkage is 
crucial for assessing how changes in market dynamics impact broader financial stability.  
By contrast, the literature investigating the effect of digital NBFIs entities is still at an early stage. In particular, 
there  is very little recent research broadening the discussion on competition and stability to include NBFIs, 
particularly digital lenders. Leveraging on technology and alternative business models, these entities introduce 
new dimensions to financial intermedia tion. Evidence suggests that digital lending can reduce transaction 
costs, improve credit allocation, and foster financial inclusion (World Bank, 2021; OECD, 2020a,b ). At the same 
time, such innovations may heighten systemic vulnerabilities, including liquidity and operational risks (FSB, 
2019), broadly consistent with Elekdag et al. (202 5a), who document that FinTech entry in emerging markets 
tends to intensify competition and reduce systemic risk, though outcomes vary with institutional and market 
characteristics. Elekdag et al. (2025b), using a large cross -country sample of more than 10,000 financial 
intermediaries, find that greater fintech penetration is associated with lower profitability among incumbent 
financial institutions, primarily through compressed net interest margins and higher operating c osts.  
At the same time, expansion of digital lending may reflect both supply -side and demand -side forces. On the 
supply side, technological innovation, declining information costs, and regulatory developments have facilitated 
the growth of fintech and other digi tal lenders. On the demand side, digital lending may expand when 
incumbent banks tighten credit standards or when previously underserved borrowers seek alternative financing

The Financial Landscape  
In the Mexican context, digital banks refer to institutions authorized by the CNBV to operate as banks while 
delivering services through fully digital channels and without physical branches . Within this broader digital 
banking ecosystem, two institution types are particularly relevant. First,  SOFIPOs —which are licensed 
microcredit institutions under the Law of Popular Savings and Credit (LACP, its acronym in Spanish) and 
supervised by the CNBV —occupy a distinct position , as they are authorized to take deposits and provide credit 
while increasingly relying on digital channels.  Second, FinTechs which are rather regulated by the 2018 
FinTech Law and  only comprise electronic payment institutions and crowdfunding platforms and do not engage 
in deposit -taking or traditional lending. While SOFIPOs are increasingly integrated into the digital banking 
space, their regulatory framework —particularly in terms of capital requirements and operational scope —differs 
markedly from that of commercial banks, as well as  FinTech firms (see Table 1).  
While both FinTechs and SOFIPOs contribute to financial inclusion and innovation, they operate under distinct 
regulatory regimes that shape their market roles, consumer protections, and licensing requirements. FinTechs  
are primarily focused on digital payments, crowdfunding, and financial infrastructure. They are not authorized to 
engage in traditional financial intermediation. Their business models often rely on partnerships with banks or 
other licensed entities to off er broader services. SOFIPOs, on the other hand, are permitted to take deposits 
and extend credit, positioning them closer to traditional banks in terms of functionality.  FinTech operations are 
subject to specific thresholds based on transaction volumes a nd user numbers. FinTechs that exceed these 
thresholds must obtain formal authorization, while those below may operate under lighter oversight or within a 
Regulatory Sandbox. The Sandbox mechanism allows FinTechs  to test innovative models under CNBV 
supervision, fostering experimentation while maintaining regulatory control. In contrast, SOFIPOs are also 
supervised by CNBV but under a separate legal framework (i.e., LACP). This law was tailored to institutions 
serving low –income and underserved populations. SOFIPOs must meet minimum capital requirements and 
adhere to prudential norms, but these are generally less stringent than those applied to commercial banks,

 The regulatory asymmetry between banks, FinTechs  and SOFIPOs has implications for market competition 
and innovation. FinTechs often operate under lighter regulatory burdens, enabling them to scale rapidly and 
offer competitive services. Conversely, SOFIPOs face more traditional oversight as these can ra ise deposits 
from the public, but benefit from operational flexibility (and lower compliance costs relative to banks, not to 
FinTechs ). This asymmetry has prompted several SOFIPOs to transition to a full banking license. Consumer 
protection standards apply equally to FinTechs and SOFIPOs. Both are required to implement anti -money 
laundering (AML) and know -your-customer protocols, as well  as cybersecurity safeguards. In Mexico, the

Data  
This paper uses a panel quarterly dataset of 53 Mexican commercial banks and eight D -SOFIPOs covering the 
period from 2018Q1 to 2024Q4.4 The eight D-SOFIPOs  in the sample represents 78 percent of the total loan 
amount in the SOFIPO sector. The banking sample represents over 99 percent of total assets of the Mexican 
commercial banking sector. A few small -sized banks were excluded from the sample because they did not 
have the necessary coverage. This exclusion was essential to maintain the consistency of the analysis, 
particularly for assessing indicators such as the Z -score, which relies on variations within a defined time frame. 
Bank -level variables were obtained from  audited quarterly financial statements (i.e., which are publicly 
available), whereas macroeconomic variables were sourced from the domestic government’s publicly available 
data. There were three mergers and acquisitions during this period. We follow the l iterature (e.g., Jiménez et 
al., 2013) and treat banks as individual entities before the acquisition took place and  created a new bank entity 
following the acquisition. Since we need a window of two years of data to compute our dependent variable’s 
first observation, we dropped the three new merged banks from our sample, due to an insufficient number of 
observations fo llowing the acquisition.

Variable Selections  
We analyze four dependent variables at the bank level that measure different dimensions of risk  and 
competition . We first use the NIM as a bank -level measure of competition pressure.  We then consider two 
popular bank -level risk measures, namely, the Z -score and the non -performing loan (NPL) ratio. The former 
reflects the bank’s ex -ante risk -taking profile, whereas the latter is linked to realized individual credit risk and 
captures the bank’s ex-post risk -taking profile. Both measures are based on disaggregated banks’ accounting 
data. While these measures provide useful insights into stand -alone risk, they do not account for important 
systemic dimensions, such as the role of the size of the ins titution, the level of interconnectedness in the 
interbank market, and the degree of substitutability. To capture these broader dynamics, we complement these 
traditional indicators with SRISK, a bank -level measure that reflects potential contributions to s ystemic risk 
under stress scenarios.

Stability  Analysis: Assessing the effect of Nu Bank  
As evidenced by the inflection point on Figure 1, the entry of Nu in the Mexican digital NBFI market constitutes 
a clear break -in-trend in the sector’s growth trajectory. To account for this shift, we split the sample to examine 
whether the baseline relationship between exposure to digital lenders and bank risk ‑taking is present across 
distinct competitive regimes  (“pre-Nu” and “post -Nu” entry) . Specifically, we re -estimate the baseline 
specifications within each subsample, maintaining the same set of outcome variables and fixed effect’s  
structure. Compared to interaction -based specifications  (i.e., an interaction dummy)  sub-sample estimation 
avoids imposing a specific functional form on size -dependent responses and allows coefficients to vary flexibly 
across bank groups.  In this way, we aim to capture potential changes in the magnitude and significance of the 
estimated coefficients attributable to Nu’s market entry, thereby providing a stability assessment of our baseline 
estimates . Panel s A and B in Table 5 show the results for the pre - and post -Nu entry, respectively .  
Importantly, t hese results  should be interpreted as reflecting changes in the dominant transmission channel 
over time, rather than as formal evidence of heterogeneous effects.  In the pre ‑Nu period (2018Q1 –2021Q4), 
DLEI exposure is associated with higher individual bank risk ‑taking, as measured by the Z ‑score, while there 
are no significant effects on pricing or competitive pressure indicators. By contrast, in the post ‑Nu entry period 
(2022Q1 –2024Q4), DL EI exposure is associated with a decline in  both the average and standard deviation of 
net interest margin s. This pattern is consistent with stronger competitive pressures following the entry of a large 
digital lender and suggests that banks may have adjusted to a more competitive environment characterized by 
lower and more stable intermediation margins. At the same time, we continue to find statistically significant 
associations between DLEI and our bank risk measure s.. Taken together, these results suggest that the 
association between DLEI and bank outcomes changed  overtime , consistent with a shift in the competitive 
environment . 
One possible explanation is that compositional effects across bank size matter,  as large and smaller banks in 
Mexico differ markedly in business models, diversification, and regulatory constraints, which may shape how 
competitive pressures from digital banks  entry transmit to balance ‑sheet risk. To explore this dimension, we 
estimate the same specification separately for large and for medium ‑ and small ‑sized banks. Table  6 reports 
the results for the pre ‑Nu period, while Table  7 presents the corresponding estimates for the post ‑Nu period.

Conclusions  
This paper investigates the impact of the expansion of alternative digital lending by non -bank financial 
intermediaries (SOFIPOs) on competitive dynamics , bank -level risk, and systemic stability within the Mexican 
banking sector. Our findings indicate that increased digital lending is associated with stronger competitive 
pressures  within the banking sector.18 This heightened competitive dynamic is especially evident among 
medium - and small -sized banks, which appear to experience greater pressure in response to the entry and 
expansion of digital NBFIs , consistent with their more limited diversification and stronger reliance on lending 
segments directly contested by digital platforms.  In addition, the evidence suggests  that the growth of digital 
lending is associated with a reduction in individual banks’ risk -taking. Specifically , higher digital lending 
penetration is associated  with more conservative ex-ante measures of bank risk-taking . Importantly, we do not 
find evidence of a statistically significant association between digital lending expansion and systemic risk , 
suggesting that the overall stability of the banking sector remains unaffected by these developments within 
Mexico’s financial system  (see IMF, 2025) , given the small and weakly interconnected nature of digital 
SOFIPOs .

 A key limitation of this analysis is that lending activity —particularly digital lending —is not fully regulated in 
Mexico. As a result, some non ‑regulated digital lenders that may compete with digital SOFIPOs are not 
captured in our dataset, reflecting the absence of publicly available information on their balance sheets or loan 
portfolios. Disclosure for such entities typically arises only when th ey are publicly listed or issue debt in capital 
markets, constraining the empirical coverage of the broader digital lending landscape. Consequently, our 
analysis focuses on the regulated segment of the market, namely SOFIPOs. This limitation, however, is 
mitigated by the fact that Nu Bank —by far the largest digital lender in Mexico’s non-bank  financial sector —is 
included in the sample and accounts for the dominant share of digital SOFIPO activity. As such, the analysis 
captures the most economically relevant segment of digital non-bank  lending in Mexico.  
Our results open several avenues for future research. For instance, our analysis can be extended to other 
emerging economies undergoing a digital transformation in their financial systems, where similar dynamics may 
influence financial stability. Second, i t would be valuable to examine banks’ responses to the entry of other 
types of digital NBFIs such as – in the case of Mexico – FinTech firms specializing in crowdfunding or 
electronic payments, and, more broadly, large digital platforms and bigtech firms , once data availability permits.  
Such institutions may affect competition and financial intermediation through channels that differ from those 
examined in our paper and could potentially amplify some of the effects documented here (Basel Committee on 
Banking Supervision, 2024).  While these questions are highly relevant, data limitations currently preclude a 
fully systematic assessment of these institutions . However,  understanding their implications for competition and 
financial stability represents an important avenue for future research.
