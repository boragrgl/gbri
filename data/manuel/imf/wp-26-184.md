ABSTRACT:  This paper  analyzes the credit -growth nexus by shifting the focus from aggregate leverage and 
credit stocks to new credit flows . Using quarterly data for 12 euro area countries over 2007 –24, covering 96 
percent of euro area GDP, the analysis shows a robust empirical association between newly granted bank credit 
and private final domestic demand (PFDD), a close proxy for GDP.  A 10 percent increase in new private credit is 
associated with about 0.5 –0.7 percentage points growth in PFDD. In contrast, specifications based on credit 
stocks or leverage produce unstable or counterintuitive estimates, reflecting measurement biases related to 
debt repayments and denominator effects. Nothwithstanding the importance of debt levels and leverage for 
financial stability  and through debt service for the economy , the findings suggest that new credit flows appear 
to provide a more empirically reliable proxy for the macroeconomic role of bank lending .

 Introduction  
The relationship between finance and economic growth , often measured by GDP,  has long occupied a 
central place in political economy, yet consensus on its nature  remains elusive . Early contributions 
established a broadly positive view, emphasizing the role of banking systems in mobilizing savings, financing 
investment, and supporting structural transformation (King and Levine 1993; Beck et al. 2000). In contrast, more 
recent lite rature —motivated by the rapid expansion of private debt in advanced economies —has increasingly 
questioned whether finance continues to support growth once economies reach high levels of leverage. A 
prominent strand argues that beyond certain thresholds, pr ivate credit becomes a drag on economic 
performance, amplifying macroeconomic volatility and generating persistent output losses (Arcand et al. 2015; 
Schularick and Taylor 2012; Milan et al. 2020; Vasconcelos et al. 2021 ). 
 
This shift from “finance as engine” to “finance as constraint” has been particularly influential in debates 
on economies where private credit exceeds 100 percent of GDP.  Empirical findings, however, remain deeply 
inconsistent. Estimates of the elasticity of output or demand with respect to credit range from strongly positive 
to statistically insignificant to sharply negative, often depending on sample periods, country set s, and 
econometric specifications  (see Literature Review below) . Within demand -led growth traditions, these results 
have been interpreted as evidence of distinct accumulation regimes —ranging from credit -led expansions driven 
by household borrowing to debt -overhang regimes , in which servicing past liabilities suppresses demand 
(Stockhammer and Wildauer 2016). Yet the empirical foundations of these claims warrant closer scrutiny.  
 
This paper argues that much of the ambiguity in the finance –growth literature arises not from genuine 
regime differences but from how credit is measured.  The prevailing reliance on credit stocks or leverage 
ratios conflates fundamentally different economic processes. The stock of private debt reflects the accumulation 
of past lending  (net of repayments ), while leverage ratios , in addition,  embed denominator effects driven by 
fluctuations in nominal GDP. As a result, conventional indicators mix the financing of current expenditure (new 
credit) with the repayment of past obligations, obscuring the role of finance in supporting effective demand. In 
extreme cases, they can generate counterintuitive inferences —for example, suggesting that deleveraging 
caused by GDP growth depresses economic activity or that unchanged leverage implies no contribution of 
credit to growth.  
 
From a political -economy perspective, this conflation is problematic.  Credit is not a passive stock  but an 
active, purposive flow mobilized to finance consumption and investment decisions in real time —a mechanism 
emphasized  by Mumtaz (2018), Altavilla et al. (2019), and Cavallo et al. (2024). Newly granted credit is closely 
aligned with and plausibly supports  private expenditure, while debt repayments come from  income generated 
by past activity and should  not constitute autonomous demand.1 Treating these two flows symmetrically risks 
misdiagnosing both the macroeconomic role of finance and the sources of demand weakness in advanced 
economies. Doing so may overstate claims of “too much finance” by attributing stagnation to the level of 
indebtedness rather than  to the pace and composition of new lending.  
    
1 “New credit flows” refers to gross disbursements of new loans, excluding debt repayments. This differs from net flows 
(disbursements minus repayments), which equal changes in credit stocks.

 Building on this insight, th is paper adopts a financing -based, flow -oriented approach to macrofinance.  
Instead of explaining GDP growth through aggregate leverage or changes in credit stocks, it focuses on new 
private bank credit —the flow of newly granted loans —as the relevant indicator linking credit finance to 
effective demand. The analysis centers on private final domestic demand (PFDD), decomposed into private 
consumption, household investment, and corporate investment. This focus aligns naturally with demand -driven  
macroeconomic frameworks, in which production responds to expenditure s, co-determined by  access to 
finance conditions.  
 
Empirically, the paper examines quarterly data for 12 euro area countries —Austria, Belgium, Estonia, 
Finland, France, Germany, Greece, Ireland, Italy, Netherlands, Portugal, and Spain —over the period 
2007 –24, covering nearly the entirety of euro area output.  The euro area sample is characterized by 
bank -based financial systems, persistently high private debt ratios, and prolonged post -Global Financial Crisis 
adjustment marked by subdued lending dynamics. Within this environment, the paper estimates the 
contribution of new credit flows to the financing of private expenditure and investment and contrasts these 
results with those obtained using repayments of past loans and conventional stock - and leverage -based 
measures.  
 
The findings yield three central results.  First, new private credit flows are robustly and positively associated 
with private demand, even in economies commonly classified as financially saturated. Although reduced -form 
correlations rather than explicit causality, a  10 percent increase in new private credit is associated with  0.5–0.7- 
percent age-point  growth in PFDD —a magnitude that is economically meaningful  and in the ballpark of 
estimates using exogenous credit supply shocks (Mumtaz 2018 ; Altavilla et al. 2019 ; Cavallo et al. 2024 ). Second, 
the effects depend  critically on the composition  of credit . New consumer credit is more strongly associated with 
consumption expenditure  than mortgage and corporate lending, the latter two reflecting their frequent use in 
financing existing assets and working capital rather than new productive capacity. Third, regressions based on 
credit stocks or leverage ratios produce unstable or unintuitive  results —often negative  elasticities —driven by 
repayment dynamics and denominator effects rather than by the financing of current demand.  
 
These results have broader implications for debates on credit and demand regimes.  They suggest that 
negative correlations between leverage and growth commonly interpreted as evidence of debt overhang may 
instead reflect periods of strong income growth or accelerated debt repayment s rather than an inherently 
contractionary role of credit. From this perspective, the macroeconomic relevance of finance lies not only in the 
level of accumulated debt but in the pace, composition, and allocation of new lending. Stagnation in highly 
indebted economies may therefore be better understood as a problem of weak credit creation and constrained 
demand rather than excessive finan ce per se.  By reorienting the analysis of credit from stocks to new credit 
flows, the paper contributes to a more coherent understanding of macrofinance within demand -driven  political 
economy. It offers a framework that helps reconcile divergent empirical findings in the finance –growth 
literature while providing a clearer accounting of how financial systems support —or fail to support —economic 
activity in advanced market  economies.

 Literature Review  
The empirical evidence is inconclusive, with results for bank credit impact on activity ranging from 
significantly positive, through nil, to sizably negative.  This ambiguity persists across various target variables, 
whether researchers are measuring real GDP or aggregate productivity :  
 
▪ Studies using aggregate leverage —the credit  stock -to-GDP ratio —as a proxy for financial 
intermediation often find higher yet more volatile elasticities. For instance, Beck et al. (2000) reported a 
high positive elasticity of 2.2 on real GDP growth, and Angeles (2015), using corporate credit -to-GDP, 
estimated elasticity of 0.6. However, these are contrasted by Koutima -Banzouzi  et al. (2021), who found 
a high negative elasticity of -2.2, and Angeles (2015), who noted a -0.4 elasticity for household credit -
to-GDP.  
 
▪ Research examining credit growth —the annual change in stock of credit —tends to yield more 
conservative or neutral results. Navaratnam and Weerasinghe (2025) found a modest elasticity of 0.06, 
while Stockhammer and Wildauer (2016) u sed changes in the credit stock to show  a decoupled effect: 
a weak positive elasticity for consumption (0.1) and a negative impact on investment ( -0.1).  
 
▪ Finally, there are several studies  that found  no significant impact (Mian et al. 2017; Naula et al. 2025) or 
those reporting conflicting impact on aggregate productivity , ranging from positive gains in 
developing contexts (Arcand et al. 2015; Abbas et al. 2022) to the large negative elasticity of -2.1 by 
Cecchetti and Kharroubi (2019) in highly  leveraged advanced countries.  
 
To reconcile these divergent findings, recent studies explored non-linearity, but biases inherent in the 
use of traditional credit metrics should  be considered.  Within non -linear frameworks, results are viewed as 
different stages of a single developmental trajectory: credit acts as a vital mobilization tool during a 
convergence phase at lower levels of financial development (Abbas et al. 2022) but eventually hits  a saturation 
point —identified by Arcand et al. (2015) at roughly 100 percent of GDP —where the costs of debt servicing 
begin to outweigh marginal benefits. Even in instances where the relationship remains technically positive, such 
as in the findings of Koursaros et al. (2021), the intensity of the effect is shown to drop sharply after country -
specific aggregate leverage thresholds. However, it is essential to recognize that simply modeling a non -
monotonic path does not resolve the fundamental biases introduced by using the stock of credit or aggregate 
leverage. These metrics remain inherently flawed because they do not account for the basic economic principle 
that credit is an active, purposive instrument; if credit is taken, it is for the explicit purpose of financing output, 
specifically through current consumption or investment. By treating credit as a passive stock r ather than the 
active tool to boost private expenditure, traditional models miss the underlying flow dynamic that links financial 
intermediation to economic  activity.

 Bank  Credit -Financ ed Activity  
Bank Credit Financing of Private Final Domestic Demand  
 
Following the literature, this paper focuses on the effects of bank credit on activity in the  non-financial 
private sector . Bank credit is an important catalyst for economic activity, providing the financing  for additional 
consumption and investment that current income levels cannot support. Consistent with established literature 
(for example , Beck et al. 2000; Arcand et al. 2015 ), this analysis focuses on the non -financial private sector, as it 
is the primary sector  of both credit absorption and economic output.  In the sample of 12 euro area countries 
(2007 –24), bank loans to households and non -financial corporates represent 72  percent  of all outstanding 
credit  (Figure 1) . In addition , this sector —comprising private consumption and private gross fixed capital 
formation (GFCF ), that is , the private final domestic demand (PFDD) —accounts on the sample average for 71  
percent  of GDP  (Figure 1)  and for 95  percent  of GDP growth  (Figure 2) . While PFDD tracks closely overall 
economic activity, the analysis and results should be interpreted as informing the demand component of GDP 
rather than aggregate growth.

 The Case for New Credit

highlights new credit flows as the  most  direct metric for capturing the impact of credit on activity . Newly 
granted credit (new business) is used to pay for goods and services and thus it is the most direct measure of

yardsticks , their effects on activity are mostly indirect, through their impact on the dynamic of newly granted 
credit  (via debt service burdens) . For instance, high leverage may slow new private credit growth.

The private consumption and investment models fit data well  and exhibit robust  cointegration . All

Estimation Results: Credit Stocks and Leverage  
Equations (1) to (3) above  were estimated in real terms (deflated by the GDP deflator) and in logarithms , 
and alternative measures of credit intermediation  were considered . Using variables in real terms directly 
and logarithmic transformation replicates the other often -used approach in literature ( for example , Beck et al. 
2000; Angeles 2015; Koutima -Banzouzi et al. 2021). For each equation, two alternative specifications were 
considered, replacing new credit with the stock of private credit and leverage (the stock of private credit -to-
GDP ratio) , respectively . Since all three variables are interlinked, the alternative specifications enable  
understanding the differences in using new credit, change in stock of credit, and aggregate leverage  when 
explaining private demand :  
▪Growth in n ew credit is the easiest to interpret, because  new credit flow s are used directly to finance
components of PFDD .
▪Growth in the stock of credit is, however, a result of interaction between new credit flows and
repayments  of past loans . As such, the resulting elasticity may turn  out to be positive , statistically
insignificant,  or negative, depending on the dynamics between new credit flows and repayments  of
past debt .
▪Finally, the sign of the elasticity of PFDD to the changes  in the aggregate leverage ratio is also possibly
ambiguous , depending on what drives the changes in the leverage ratio —the numerator ( stock of
credit ) or the denominator ( GDP growth ).

 The findings for new real credit show similar results to the baseline specification (Tab le 6). A percentage 
increase in new real consumer and mortgage credit is associated with growth of the real private consumption 
and household investment by 0.05 and 0.09 percentage points, respectively. The elasticity of demand for new 
real corporate credit was statistically insignificant, though  it remained positive and significant in the 
instrumental variable regression that aims to address potential endogeneity of new credit and demand by using 
lending surveys as instruments (Table 7). Using lending surveys (on credit standards) is particularly useful 
because  they correlate with new credit series but remain largely exogenous to demand , as credit standards are 
set by banks. Although they do not fully isolat e causal effects, the results are consistent with plausibl e 
exogenous shifts in credit supply and present stronger results compared to the baseline regression. Overall, in 
line with the baseline specification, these results confirm that a 10 percent increase in real private (household 
and corporate) credit correlates with a 0.5 -0.6 percentage points to growth of real PFDD.  
 
Elasticities to stocks of real credit were  statistically insignificant  or positive, highlighting the role of 
repayments of past loans in driving its relation to PFDD. Statistically insignificant elasticities for household 
investment and private final consumption to real credit stock are likely a result of increasing household debt 
repayments, considering  elasticities of household investment and final consumption to new credit were positive 
and statistically significant (similar for corporate investment ). The positive and significant elasticity of corporate 
investment to stock of credit contrasts with the insignificant elasticity to new credit, highlighting the impact of 
corporate debt repayment s. Stocks of debt and new credit flows thus offer distinct perspectives : new loans 
matter for understanding the link between credit and PFDD  growth, while debt stocks remain relevant for 
balance sheet  analyses , financial stability , and the impact of the debt service burden on new credit growth .       
 
Leverage appears to be negatively related to  PFDD, but this is driven by the denominator of the leverage 
ratio. Elasticities of household consumption and investment to leverage were statistically significantly negative . 
This may appear to be consistent with findings that above 100 percent private credit -to-GDP (nearly all 
countries in the sample period maintained a ratio above 100 percent), increasing leverage is a drag on  growth 
(Arcand et al. 2015). However, the insignificant results using the stock of credit (the n umerator  of leverage) and 
the statistically insignificant elasticity of corporate investment to leverage suggest that th ese correlation s are 
driven by GDP growth (the denominator of leverage) and do not represent a reliable, stable relation but rather 
an episode -dependent correlation that is misrepresenting the effect of credit on output. This may also explain 
the mixed and sample -dependent findings (positive, insignificant, and negative elasticities) in literature when 
using aggregate leverage to explain GDP growth.   
 
 
Conclusion  
This paper revisit s the relationship between bank credit and economic activity from  a financing 
perspective , emphasiz ing new private credit flows over  changes in credit stocks or aggregate leverage.  
Using quarterly data for 12 euro area countries over 2007 –24, the analysis shows that newly granted private 
credit is robustly correlated with private final domestic demand (PFDD), a close proxy for GDP. A key finding is

 that a 10 percent increase in new private credit is associated with  PFDD growth by about 0.5 -0.7 percentage 
points, a magnitude that is stable across specifications and economically meaningful . 
 
The results highlight the limitations of traditional measures of financial intermediation  and robustly 
show the significance of new credit for private demand . Credit stocks and the leverage ratio combine new 
lending with repayments of past debt , and leverage is further affected by fluctuations in nominal GDP  in the 
denominator . Results show that repayments of past loans do not significantly correlate with private demand. 
Consequently , private demand regressions based on these indicators often yield unstable or counterintuitive 
estimates, including negative elasticities that are difficult to reconcile with the financing role of credit. In 
contrast, focusing on new credit can better isolate the flow of financing that is most closely associated with 
current consumption and investment decisions, offering a more theoretically coherent and empirically reliable 
measure of credit’s macroeconomic role.   
 
Disaggregated estimates shed light on the transmission  channels.  New consumer credit translates most 
directly into private consumption, while new mortgage and corporate lending have a more muted link to 
investment, reflecting their frequent use in financing existing dwellings  and working capital , respectively . 
Consistent with this composition, the overall contribution of credit to demand growth during the post -Global 
Financial Crisis period was modest, reflecting subdued lending dynamics rather than diminished  effectiveness of 
credit. Cross -country heterogeneity further underscores the importance of new credit flows: countries with 
more resilient new lending experienced stronger demand support  from credit , while those facing prolonged 
contractions in new credit recorded weaker demand  outcomes.  
 
This paper also shows that the estimated effects of new credit flows are closely aligned with those 
derived from  exogenous credit -supply shocks identified using  lending standards or bond spreads.  This 
convergence suggests that new credit volumes provide a practical proxy for underlying credit conditions, 
capturing the macrofinancial transmission of supply shocks without the need for complex identification 
strategies.  
 
The o verall  findings suggest that the pace and composition of new lending  are more informative for 
understanding the credit -growth nexus  than  change s in the  level of accumulated debt.  While debt levels 
and leverage remain critical for financial stabilit y and potentially affect activity , including the pace of new 
lending  through debt service burden, analyzing the credit -growth  nexus  would benefit from a greater focus on 
indicators of new credit creation and allocation. Such an approach may offer  a clearer lens for assessing the role 
of bank lending in economic activity.
