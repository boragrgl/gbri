Financial markets have been resilient since the April 
2026 Global Financial Stability Report, but financial 
stability risks are elevated. Markets have found support 
from strong corporate earnings and solid economic 
growth, as high investments in firms related to artificial 
intelligence (AI) have helped offset a possible drag from 
geopolitical tensions and policy uncertainty. On balance, 
financial conditions remain accommodative. 
At the same time, this report highlights three salient 
risks that could be near-term catalysts for a sharp tighten -
ing of financial conditions and market turmoil, selected based on the likelihood of their triggering shocks and the severity of their potential market impact. 
First, although core firms in the AI ecosystem have 
been profitable so far—easing pressure on AI-related 
equity valuations—weaker or less durable investment and 
profitability in AI could trigger a correction in AI-related 
asset prices, exacerbated by rising leverage, concentrated 
exposures, and circular financing arrangements. Juris -
dictions with financial markets sensitive to global AI developments, as well as those with substantial holdings 
of AI assets, are likely to be more exposed to negative 
wealth effects or sharp market corrections.  
Second, resurgent inflation or fiscal concerns could 
trigger a sharp rise in sovereign bond yields and spark broader market turmoil given the foundational impor-
tance of sovereign bonds. High public debt and rollover 
risks remain unresolved, increasing the chances that a 
rise in yields could occur in a disorderly manner, despite 
stable bond market liquidity and the recent unwinding of some leveraged trades.
Third, cyber threats constitute a growing systemic risk 
as financial services become more digital and intercon-
nected. For their part, frontier AI models are emerging 
as both powerful enablers of cyberattacks and critical 
defensive tools.
These risks by themselves do not necessarily threaten 
financial stability, but they could become a systemic 
risk—impairments to key financial intermediation and 
credit provision, with serious negative economic effects—
if they are amplified by financial vulnerabilities. This 
report provides updated assessments on key vulnerabili-
ties and presents new analyses of emerging ones.Strong core balance sheets across banks and house-
holds have aided the resilience of financial markets. 
A global stress test confirms that banks would remain 
broadly resilient under an adverse scenario featuring an 
energy price shock, higher inflation and bond yields, and 
an AI-led market correction. However, such a scenario 
would also reveal a significant share of vulnerable banks. 
Households’ debt burdens have declined across countries 
in recent years, while their net worth has increased, mak -
ing them less likely to be a source of systemic risk. That said, a correction in housing or equity prices could curb 
household consumption and disproportionately impact lower-income households. 
In emerging market economies, policy buffers have 
eroded in some cases, while the greater reliance of 
frontier economies on opaque alternative financing could 
weaken debt transparency and hinder future market 
access. 
Several other vulnerabilities that could amplify risks 
have worsened. Insurance and pension funds have grown 
their holdings of illiquid assets, increasing liquidity mis-
match risks and the likelihood of forced asset sales during 
market stress. Higher borrower leverage and growing contingent liquidity risks faced by private direct lending 
funds could sharply curtail credit when financial condi-
tions tighten, a test the sector has not yet faced. Among 
nonbank financial intermediaries (NBFIs), although 
the growing hedge fund sector tends to support market 
functioning in normal times, its use of leverage and pur-
suit of crowded trades could amplify market stress, with spillovers to prime brokers and broader financial markets 
(Chapter 2). Finally, although tokenized assets remain 
small and could offer efficiency gains in several areas, 
they can create new channels for the buildup of leverage 
and mismatches in liquidity and maturity, in addition to 
elevating risks related to technology and infrastructure 
(Chapter 3).

room for macroeconomic policy support, should focus 
on resilience, transparency, and preparedness. Authorities

Executive Directors broadly agreed with staff’s 
assessment of the outlook, risks, and policy priorities. 
They concurred that the global economy is again proving resilient despite repeated shocks, even as the interaction of geopolitics and technology has produced a split global economy. Favorable terms of trade and technology related exports and investment provide a cushion to energy exporters outside the conflict region and economies with strong participation in the technology upturn. At the same time, energy import-ing economies with limited exposure to the technology value chain, including many low income and vulner-able countries, face stronger headwinds, underscoring the importance of continued Fund support. Looking ahead, uncertainties surrounding the sustainability of the margins of adjustment that have supported resilience underscore the need to address underlying vulnerabilities and rebuild buffers. 
Directors broadly concurred that risks to the out-
look remain skewed to the downside. They stressed that prolongation of wars could revive inflationary pressures, worsen fiscal and external balances, and weigh on growth, with additional disruptions to infrastructure, cyberattacks, or extreme weather events potentially amplifying the energy shock, and any renewed trade tensions adding to inflationary pressures. A reassessment of AI related prospects could sharply reduce investment, trigger equity market corrections, and tighten financial conditions, with global spillovers. Directors also noted that resurgent inflation or fiscal concerns could trigger a sharp rise in sovereign bond yields and spark broader market turmoil given the foundational importance of sovereign bonds. Cyber threats constitute a growing systemic risk as financial services become more digital and interconnected. These downside risks could be amplified by struc-tural vulnerabilities in the financial sector—including elevated leverage and growing liquidity mismatches, especially among nonbank financial institutions—lead-ing to systemic risks. Directors generally acknowledged the upside risks, including continued momentum in the technology cycle, faster than assumed productivity gains from AI, more predictable trade policies, and more resilient energy sources and supply chains. 
Directors called for a renewed focus on rebuilding 
fiscal space amid more limited room to maneuver due to elevated debt levels, rising borrowing costs, and mounting demands on public budgets, while empha-sizing the importance of tailoring policy responses to country circumstances. They stressed that fiscal policy should be designed in a way not to complicate the task of monetary policy and that, where support for the most vulnerable is necessary, it should be transparent, temporary, and targeted, with budgetary offsets specified upfront, especially where fiscal space is constrained. Directors noted that, in a shock prone world, the capacity to deliver targeted support is cru-cial, and called for strengthening delivery mechanisms that can be scaled rapidly when shocks hit, supported as needed by Fund technical assistance. Directors underscored that credible medium term consolidation should rest on well specified plans, including durable revenue measures, greater spending efficiency, and prudent debt management, supported by institutional improvements. They concurred that better tax design can support growth, and encouraged reforms to reduce distortions in the cost of capital and to recalibrate marginal rates on labor income, so as to lift investment and employment while safeguarding revenue. 
Directors reaffirmed the importance of preserving 
price and financial stability. They generally stressed that central banks should remain agile and firmly focused on their mandates, with a few Directors cautioning that monetary policy should not overreact to inflationary pressures from fiscal policies or energy shocks. Introduction
Financial markets have remained resilient since the 
April 2026 Global Financial Stability Report. Economic 
growth has held up and corporate earnings have been 
strong, particularly among firms related to artificial intelligence (AI). Likely responding to higher inflation-ary pressures stemming from the energy shock from the war in the Middle East, 14 of 33 major central banks have hiked policy rates since the second quarter of 2026, up from just 2 in the first quarter.
1 However, 
inflation has been more contained than initially feared, leading to only a modest rise in policy rates. Financial conditions tightened somewhat in the first quarter, but the subsequent tailwind from corporate valuations more than offset the tightening from higher interest rates, leaving overall financial conditions accommoda-tive on net (Figure 1.1). Non-China emerging market economies are a notable exception, with financial conditions tightening this year to date, mostly in the first quarter. 
The chapter identifies three salient risks ahead: an 
AI-driven market correction, a sharp rise in sovereign bond yields, and cyber threats. These risks are plausible near-term catalysts for a sharp tightening of financial conditions and market turmoil, selected based on the likelihood of their triggering shocks and the severity of their potential market impact. Weaker or less durable investment and profitability in AI would not only undermine a key engine of growth that has helped off-set the economic drag from the war in the Middle East and heightened policy uncertainty, but also lead to a correction in AI-linked equities and credit, which now account for a large share of both markets. Chapter 2 at a Glance
 •Hedge funds have become increasingly important participants in global financial markets, with gross assets 
more than tripling since the previous decade to about $13 trillion in early 2026.
 •The growing market footprint of hedge funds can support market liquidity and efficiency in normal times but may amplify price dislocations and liquidity strains during periods of stress.
 •This amplification is driven by key vulnerabilities, including leverage, fragile investor funding, and crowded trades in which many hedge funds hold similar positions. 
 •Equities held by more vulnerable hedge funds experience larger price declines and higher volatility during market stress.
 •Leverage amplifies the adverse effects of crowded positions and investor redemptions, including through the widening of spreads when leveraged sovereign bond trades unwind.
 •Adverse shocks to hedge funds can spill over to prime brokers, typically large dealer banks, and broader financial markets, with potential implications for macrofinancial stability.
 •Hedge fund vulnerabilities remain difficult to monitor because of significant data gaps, particularly regard-ing leverage, derivatives exposures, counterparty relationships, and cross-border activities.
Policy Recommendations
 •Close data gaps and strengthen risk monitoring, including through stress testing and scenario analysis, to better identify vulnerabilities in markets with significant hedge fund participation. 
 •Address risks from crowded trades and synchronized deleveraging through targeted and well-calibrated activity-based measures, including stronger collateral management practices. 
 •Where risks are concentrated, deploy entity-based measures, such as risk-based leverage limits, capital or margin add-ons, and limits on concentrated exposures.
 •Improve market resilience by strengthening counterparty risk management.

Although hedge funds hold a modest share of outstanding equity and debt securities, they play a central role in arbitrage and information-driven trading, which allows them to influence both the speed and magnitude of asset price adjustments. For example, hedge funds can acceler-ate the incorporation of news about technological developments into equity prices through short selling, derivatives trading, and other flexible trading strategies that enable them to adjust positions more rapidly than many traditional asset managers. 
Vulnerabilities of hedge funds influence the trans-
mission of shocks. The dual role of hedge funds in financial markets, discussed earlier as supporting effi-ciency and liquidity in calm times but potentially exac-erbating stress when conditions deteriorate, is closely linked to their vulnerabilities—notably, concentrated and crowded positions, correlated end-investor flows, and leverage. 
Concentrated and crowded exposures increase the 
sensitivity of portfolios to adverse price movements and raise the risk of synchronized unwinding. Con-centrated positions reflect large exposures to specific assets, whereas crowded positions arise when similar exposures are shared across investors. Hedge funds often concentrate investments in securities with attrac-tive risk-adjusted expected returns, and when such exposures become widespread across funds, prices can become more sensitive to their trading activity. During periods of market stress, crowded positions can unwind rapidly, leading to price declines that exceed what fundamentals alone would suggest (Boyson, Stahel, and Stulz 2010; Cai and others 2019; Brown, Howard, and Lundblad 2022). Consequently, securities heav-ily held by hedge funds may be vulnerable not only to issuer-specific risks but also to shocks from hedge fund deleveraging and herding, amplifying stress across markets and institutions (Figure 2.4).
Correlated end-investor redemptions can further 
amplify selling pressures during market stress. The impact of hedge fund sales on asset prices depends on the concentration of holdings as well as on the degree of synchronization in portfolio adjustments across funds. Securities held by a small number of hedge funds experiencing significant redemptions are par-ticularly vulnerable to price dislocations arising from forced sales. Deleveraging can force hedge funds to reduce expo-

Hedge funds rely heavily on secured financing and derivatives-based leverage, both of which are highly sensitive to market conditions through financing terms and margin requirements. As a result, hedge fund leverage is strongly procyclical (Kerry, Lewrick, and Schmieder 2026). This procyclicality can transform hedge funds from liquidity providers in calm times into demanders of liquidity during market stress (Jylhä, Rinne, and Suominen 2014), exacerbating selling pressures, asset price dislocations, and market illiquidity (Ben-David and others 2013; Brunnermeier and Oehmke 2014; Schrimpf, Shin, and Sushko 2020; FSB 2023).
Interactions among leverage, crowding, and redemp-
tion pressures can create powerful feedback loops that intensify market stress and price dislocations. For example, margin calls can trigger the unwinding of crowded positions, resulting in price declines that can generate additional margin calls and further delever-aging. Similarly, correlated investor redemptions can force leveraged funds to reduce exposures simulta-neously, especially when they target specific leverage ratios. The ensuing selling pressure may increase mar-ket volatility, with binding internal risk limits prompt-ing further portfolio adjustments. 
The extent of shock transmission from hedge funds 
to broader financial conditions depends on the market footprint and interconnectedness of the sector. Hedge funds with a significant presence across core finan -

These effects are amplified by hedge funds’ close links with other financial intermediaries, partic-ularly prime brokers. When hedge funds are forced to deleverage, prime brokers may incur losses on their hedge fund exposures. Moreover, the resulting asset sales can strain the balance sheets of prime brokers and other dealers absorbing these flows, potentially limiting their intermediation capacity because of regulatory and risk-management constraints (Duffie 2020). Conclusions and Policy 
Recommendations
Hedge funds have become increasingly important 
participants in global financial markets. During calm market conditions, hedge funds support market func-
tioning by providing liquidity, facilitating risk sharing, and correcting price dislocations. However, during periods of stress, their leverage, crowded positions, and correlated investor redemptions can interact to trigger synchronized deleveraging and asset sales, reducing market liquidity, tightening funding conditions, and amplifying shocks. Given hedge funds’ extensive links to prime brokers, derivatives counterparties, repo mar-kets, and other financial intermediaries, stress in the sector can spill across markets, impairing market func-tioning, increasing asset price volatility, and tightening financial conditions more broadly.
As the sector continues to grow, safeguarding 
macrofinancial stability requires close attention to hedge fund vulnerabilities. The findings of this chap-ter underscore the need for a comprehensive policy approach that combines improved data collection and risk monitoring, targeted prudential measures, and market-structure reforms to strengthen resilience. By identifying key hedge fund vulnerabilities and the conditions under which they can amplify shocks, the chapter helps inform targeted policy responses and systemwide surveillance.
Data, Transparency, and Risk Monitoring
Significant data gaps continue to limit the ability 
of policymakers to assess vulnerabilities and inter-connections involving hedge funds. Efforts should prioritize improving information on leverage, exposures to derivatives, securities financing transactions, prime broker relationships, and investor redemption arrange-ments. Closing these gaps would support a more comprehensive assessment of risks and their transmis-sion channels. Given the global nature of the hedge fund industry and the prominent role of offshore fund domiciles, stronger cross-border reporting frameworks and information-sharing arrangements are essential to improve risk attribution and monitoring.

works should be strengthened to better assess the resilience of markets with significant hedge fund participation. Chapter 3 at a Glance
 •Tokenization—the representation of financial assets and liabilities on distributed programmable ledgers—
has the potential to transform financial markets. By placing functions traditionally performed by separate intermediaries onto a distributed ledger, tokenization can reduce reconciliation needs, increase transpar-ency, and enable greater automation. 
 •The tokenization ecosystem is subject to strong network effects: both benefits and risks increase when more assets, participants, and settlement instruments are brought onto the ledgers.
 •The tokenized asset market remains small and fragmented. Future growth depends, among other factors, on how four key, interdependent constraints are addressed: legal certainty, regulatory clarity, interoperabil-ity, and availability of settlement assets.
 •This chapter documents novel empirical evidence on some emerging benefits and risks. T raditional financial markets, for example, for 
equities and bonds, operate through a network of 
specialized intermediaries, each maintaining a ledger and performing a distinct function along the asset life cycle. T rading, clearing, settlement, custody, servicing, and collateral management are typically conducted across separate institutions and recordkeeping systems (Figure 3.1). Although this fragmented structure can create operational complexity, delays, and costs, it also reflects legal, regulatory, and risk-management arrangements that have evolved to support market integrity and financial stability (Cabedo and others 2026).
T raditional finance benefits from strong network 
effects through a few dominant central securities depositories, central clearing counterparties, Society for Worldwide Interbank Financial Telecommunication (SWIFT), major exchanges, and custodians. Tokeni-zation attempts to recreate many of these functions on distributed ledgers. In tokenized environments, financial assets are represented as digital tokens on distributed ledgers, which allow participants to interact through a common transaction environment governed by predefined rules. More activities can be executed directly on shared infrastructure, potentially reduc-ing reconciliation needs, increasing transparency, and enabling greater automation (Box 3.1). This results in some integration or automation of traditional func-tions, which could potentially ease frictions, lower costs, and improve efficiency (Agur and others 2025). The system is subject to strong network effects: Ben-efits and risks increase when more assets, participants, and settlement instruments are brought into the plat-form and as markets converge on common standards and settlement arrangements rather than fragment into many isolated platforms.

Key Constraints to the Growth of 
Tokenized Markets 
The growth of asset tokenization is currently 
constrained by four key, interdependent factors: legal 
certainty, regulatory clarity, interoperability, and safe, sound, and scalable settlement (Figure 3.5).
Legal certainty is a prerequisite for scale, as market 
participants need confidence that tokenized assets confer enforceable rights and obligations. The legal framework must establish clear rights over both the token and the underlying asset it represents, define the legal relationship between the two, and clarify whether transfers recorded on distributed ledgers constitute legally effective transfers of ownership and settlement finality—and at what point. The framework must also address the legal recognition and enforceability of smart contracts, distributed records, and ledger entries as well as the responsibilities of custodians and other service providers. Additional risks arise in cases of insolvency, enforcement, fraud, and cyber inci-dents (see Online Annex 3.3, which provides exam-ples of specific operational and infrastructure risks). In cross-border transactions, differences in property laws and conflict-of-law rules may create uncertainty over ownership, jurisdiction, and the enforceability of claims. Without legal certainty across these dimen-sions, adoption, especially by institutional investors, is likely to remain limited.

Tokenized Markets
The pace and direction of adopting tokenization

Policy Recommendations
Tokenization is one of several innovations that could 
help address inefficiencies in financial markets. To support safe and efficient market development, poli-cymakers should adopt a technology-neutral approach that allows different technologies to compete on their merits. Removing unnecessary constraints to tokeni-zation while safeguarding financial stability is the first step toward enabling tokenized markets to develop. This implies assuring legal certainty and improving regulatory clarity, focusing on potential operational, governance, and infrastructure risks and upgrading supervisory capacity.
In emerging markets, although tokenization could 
support and complement efforts to deepen and develop capital markets, fundamental reforms remain paramount. Tokenization may allow some emerging market and developing economies to “leapfrog” legacy market infrastructure but not the broader process of capital market development itself. Where incumbent systems are less entrenched, newer arrangements, such as on-chain native bond issuance, can be adopted without replicating the infrastructure architecture of mature markets. Infrastructure, however, is only one pillar of capital market development, and the costs of adoption warrant careful consideration. Building tokenized infrastructure, adapting legal frameworks, and developing supervisory capacity require substan-tial fixed investments, whereas efficiency gains tend to increase with transaction volumes. In low-turnover markets, whether the potential gains will outweigh the costs remains uncertain. Authorities should advance the foundational elements of capital market devel-opment, including a broad investor base, benchmark pricing, secondary market liquidity, and robust legal and supervisory safeguards in parallel with technology adoption. Otherwise, tokenization may add opera-tional, cyber, and governance risks to shallow markets that lack legacy infrastructure and established institu-tional arrangements to fall back on. 
Promote Technology Neutrality by Removing 
Unnecessary Constraints
Legal Certainty
To promote technology neutrality, national author-
ities should first review their legal frameworks and 
ensure that laws provide legal certainty for tokenized assets. The law needs to establish clear rights and claims over tokens and define the legal relationship between tokenized assets and their real-world equiv-alents. It should also address key issues across the tokenized asset life cycle, including issuance, transfer, redemption, insolvency treatment, and enforcement actions, as well as jurisdictional and conflict-of-law rules in cross-border transactions. In doing so, author-ities should clarify the rights conveyed by tokenized assets, including whether they confer direct rights over the underlying asset or claims against the issuer (Garrido 2023).
23 
Regulatory Clarity
Second, financial sector authorities should provide 
regulatory clarity. In the short term, existing secu-rities, banking, and payments regulations can and should be applied to tokenized markets. Consistent with the principle of “same activity, same risk, same regulatory outcome,” international standard setters generally adopt a technology-neutral approach focused on the underlying asset, activity, and risks rather than the technology used. As a result, tokenized securities should generally be subject to securities regulation, tokenized deposits to banking regulation, and token- ized payment arrangements to payments regulation. Similarly, requirements governing issuance, disclosure, transparency, market conduct, corporate governance, best execution, market surveillance, and consumer pro-tection should continue to apply in tokenized markets. This approach helps prevent regulatory arbitrage while allowing innovation to develop within established legal and regulatory frameworks.
